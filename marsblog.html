Origins

Mars: A Lost World?

Around 4 billion years ago, Mars was not the barren wasteland we see today. It had a rich atmosphere and all the conditions necessary for life — though whether life ever existed there is still up for debate.

Mars no longer has a magnetic field because its core has cooled and solidified. This left the planet vulnerable to solar wind, which stripped away its atmosphere and transformed it into the bleak, desolate world we know today.

02
Phase One

Unlocking Trapped Oxygen

All that oxygen and water had to go somewhere — and that somewhere is now trapped in rocks and subterranean ice caps. To release them, we need to reverse the reactions that locked these gases in place through thermolysis.

In other words: we need to melt Mars's surface. Our laser would need to reach approximately 5,500°C for thermolysis to occur, releasing 750 grams of oxygen per cubic metre of Martian rock.

5,500°C Laser temperature
750g O₂ per m³
8m Surface depth

To access the water, we'd need to melt roughly eight metres of the Martian surface — deepening entire regions of ancient lake beds back into existence.

03
Phase Two

The Great Superstorm

Once we've incinerated the Martian surface, it will look frightening. Storms will rage, raining down vaporised water. This tempest could last for decades, eventually forming lakes and rivers.

A strange type of snow composed mainly of iron and silicon will fall from the sky, formed from materials that vaporised during the melting. This process will also wash away many toxic gases and harmful compounds — a violent but necessary cleanse.

04
Phase Three

Nitrogen from Titan

The atmosphere is over 95% oxygen — dangerously flammable and unbreathable. To make it Earth-like, we need nitrogen. And we know exactly where to get it.

Titan, one of Saturn's moons, has an atmosphere composed of 90% nitrogen. Automated factories would liquefy it, then launch it toward Mars via mass drivers — machines that accelerate payloads past 2 km/s, letting them detonate on impact.

90% Titan N₂ atmosphere
2 km/s Launch velocity

Saturn looms in Titan's sky as automated mass drivers hurl canisters of liquid nitrogen across the solar system — aimed at a planet not yet alive, but waiting to be.

05
Phase Four

Seeding Life

After nearly a century of work, Mars would have all the conditions necessary for life. The journey of life on Earth began with phytoplankton, and it would start no differently on Mars — we seed the oceans with vast quantities of these microscopic organisms.

With abundant resources, they would quickly populate, forming the base of the food chain. Gradually we introduce zooplankton, then fish — and with relatively little intervention, the oceans would become a thriving ecosystem.

Achieving a stable land ecosystem is far trickier — plants need soil. We could wait millennia for water and wind to naturally create it, or pulse our laser repeatedly, causing rock to shatter. Add water, and we get mud.

The best plants for the Martian environment are those native to volcanic regions on Earth. In Mars's lesser gravity, trees could grow to extraordinary heights — their roots breaking more rock into soil, creating a self-sustaining cycle.

06
Final Phase

The Solar Shield

Mars still lacks a magnetic field, leaving it vulnerable to solar wind and radiation. The solution: a superconductive ring stationed between Mars and the Sun, powered by nuclear energy, generating an artificial magnetosphere.

With these steps completed, Mars would no longer be a barren wasteland. It would be a thriving, living world — humanity's second home, ready for the next great adventure.
