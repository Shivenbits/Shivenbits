CHAPTER 01

Mars: A Lost World?

Around 4 billion years ago, Mars was not the barren wasteland we see today. In fact, it had a pleasant, oxygen-rich atmosphere and all the conditions necessary for life — though whether life ever existed there is still up for debate.

So, what happened to all that? Mars no longer has a magnetic field because its core, although made of a similar composition to Earth's, has cooled and solidified. This left the red planet vulnerable to solar wind and radiation, which caused it to lose its atmosphere and become the bleak, desolate place we know today.

CHAPTER 02

Unlocking Trapped Oxygen & Water

All that oxygen and water had to go somewhere — and that somewhere is now trapped in rocks and subterranean ice caps. To release these, we need to reverse the reactions that lock these gases in place through thermolysis. In other words, we need to melt Mars's surface.

Our laser would need to reach 5 500 °C for thermolysis to occur, releasing 750 g/m³ of oxygen from Martian rock. To access the water, we'd need to melt roughly 8 m depth of the Martian surface, deepening regions into nascent lakes and rivers.

CHAPTER 03

The Great Storm

Once the Martian surface has been incinerated by our laser network, it will look frightening. Storms will rage, raining down water vaporised during the process. These storms could last for decades, eventually forming lakes and rivers across the new landscape.

"A strange iron-and-silicon snow will fall from the sky — material vaporised during the melt, now precipitating back as metallic sleet washing away toxic compounds."

This process will also wash away many toxic gases and harmful compounds — a violent planetary cleaning that sets the stage for everything that follows.

CHAPTER 04

Nitrogen — Borrowed from Titan

At this stage the atmosphere is highly flammable — over 95% O₂ — and lacks the nitrogen buffer required to sustain life. To correct this, we look 1.4 billion kilometres away: Titan, one of Saturn's moons, whose atmosphere is composed of approximately 90% N₂.

CHAPTER 05

Seeding Life

After nearly a century of work, we would have restored Mars to something approaching its ancient glory. Getting life itself onto the planet, however, demands its own deliberate strategy.

The journey of life on Earth began with phytoplankton in the ocean — and it would be no different for Mars. We would seed the new oceans with vast quantities of phytoplankton. With abundant dissolved nutrients and sunlight, these organisms would rapidly colonise the ocean, forming the base of a brand-new food chain. Gradually we could introduce zooplankton, fish, and eventually apex predators.

Achieving a stable land ecosystem is much trickier — plants need soil, and Mars has none. We are left with two options: wait millennia for water and wind to naturally create it, or pulse the laser on and off repeatedly, causing rock to contract and expand rapidly, eventually shattering it. Add water, and we get mud. The best plants for the Martian environment are those native to volcanic regions on Earth.

In the lesser gravity of Mars — just 0.38g — trees could grow to previously impossible heights. Their roots would gather nutrients from deeper layers, forming more soil in a self-sustaining loop. At this point, we could begin introducing insects, then animals.

CHAPTER 06

The Sun: Our Final Challenge

Even with an atmosphere and a biosphere, Mars remains vulnerable. Without a magnetic field, solar wind will slowly strip the atmosphere again over millions of years — and ionising radiation will remain hostile to surface life.

The best solution is a solar umbrella: a vast superconductive ring, placed at the Mars–Sun L1 Lagrange point, powered by onboard nuclear reactors. This ring would generate an artificial magnetosphere, deflecting solar wind and reducing surface radiation to Earthlike levels.

Solar Shield — System Specifications
L1 Lagrange Point Orbital position between Mars and the Sun, providing continuous shadow and magnetospheric coverage
SC Superconducting Ring High-temperature superconductor loop generating the required magnetic dipole field strength
∞ Sustained Operation Nuclear power source enabling continuous operation without solar dependence

With these steps completed, Mars would no longer be a barren wasteland. It would be a thriving, living world — and the next great chapter of the human story.
