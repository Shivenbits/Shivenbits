Expected Value

The long-run weighted average per trial — calculated as $E[X] = \sum x_i \cdot P(x_i)$. Positive EV favors the player; negative EV guarantees the casino profits.

∞

Law of Large Numbers

As trials $n \to \infty$, the observed average converges to the true EV. Even 10–15 trials reveal the directional trend toward that theoretical limit.

Game A

Positive Expected Value — The Player Profits Over Time

Played with a standard 52-card deck, this game exploits the asymmetric payout between prime and composite numbers. Face cards (J, Q, K, A) trigger a redraw, creating a clean probability space of 36 eligible cards — and an edge tilted toward the player.

Rules & Structure
Cost to play: $1.00 per round
Draw 1 card from shuffled 52-card deck
Win: Prime card (2, 3, 5, 7 — 16 cards) → Payout $3.50 (net +$2.50)
Loss: Composite card (4–10 — 20 cards) → No payout (net −$1.00)
Face cards (J, Q, K, A) → Redraw

⚡ Key Takeaways — Game A

  • The $3.50 payout on a ~30.8% win rate creates a positive expected value despite losing most individual rounds.
  • Over thousands of plays, the Law of Large Numbers guarantees the player's average converges to +$0.08.
  • This models how a well-priced bet or investment can be profitable even when individual losses dominate short sessions.

Game B

Negative Expected Value — The Casino Profits Over Time

This game uses a six-sided die secretly weighted to land on '1' with 20% probability instead of the fair 16.67%. The remaining faces each carry a 16% probability — a subtle imbalance that shifts the house edge just enough to guarantee long-term casino profit.

Rules & Structure
Cost to play: $1.00 per round
Roll a weighted 6-sided die
Win: High roll (4, 5, 6) → Payout $2.00 (net +$1.00)
Loss: Low roll (1, 2, 3) → No payout (net −$1.00)
Die is weighted: P(1) = 0.20, P(2–6) = 0.16 each

⚡ Key Takeaways — Game B

  • A mere 4% shift in probability (from 50/50 to 52/48) is all the house needs to guarantee long-run profit.
  • The weighted die demonstrates how imperceptible rigging compounds into significant loss over many trials.
  • Real-world analogy: insurance companies use exactly this principle — pricing premiums so $E[\text{claim}] < \text{premium}$, ensuring long-run profit.
