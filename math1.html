Trick 01

Multiply Any Number by 11 in Seconds

Since $11$ is the first real palindromic number, I wondered whether there's a way to speed up multiplication. While experimenting, I found a pattern that makes multiplying by $11$ almost instant.

Did You Know?
$11$ is special because it's a repunit prime — a number made of repeated digits. This structure is exactly why the multiplication trick works so elegantly!
The Two-Digit Shortcut
$\overline{ab} \times 11 = \overline{a \;(a+b)\; b}$

Take any two-digit number. Add the two digits. Sandwich the sum between them. That's it! ✦

Pro Tip — Example
Take $52$. We compute $5 + 2 = 7$, then place it between the digits:
$$52 \times 11 = 5\underbrace{7}_{5+2}2 = \mathbf{572}$$ Verify: $52 \times 11 = 52 \times 10 + 52 = 520 + 52 = 572$ ✓

Trick 02

The Reversing 1089 Trick

While exploring special numbers, we discovered a surprising result — no matter which qualifying 3-digit number you pick, the same number always appears at the end: $1089$.

Did You Know?
$1089 = 33^2$. It also equals $\frac{9 \times 11 \times 11}{1}$. Some mathematicians call it a Kaprekar-type constant for 3-digit numbers!

Follow these steps with any qualifying number:

1
Pick a 3-digit number
The first and last digits must differ by at least $2$.
e.g., 521
2
Reverse the digits
Write the number backwards.
521 → 125
3
Subtract the smaller from the larger
Always subtract the smaller reversed number from the original.
521 − 125 = 396
4
Reverse the result
Flip the answer you just got.
396 → 693
5
Add the two numbers
Add the result and its reverse — and behold!
396 + 693 = 1089 ✦
⚠ Why Does It Fail Sometimes?
If the first and last digits differ by less than $2$, the intermediate subtraction doesn't produce a "valid" 3-digit difference — the mechanism breaks. Understanding exactly why is a great exercise in algebraic thinking. Let $\overline{abc}$ be the number and work through the algebra!
Deep Dive 03

Why Does Dividing by Zero Break Math?

Division has always felt intuitive — asking "how many times does one number go into another?" For example, $12 \div 3 = 4$ means $3$ goes into $12$ exactly four times. But $12 \div 0$? That asks how many times $0$ goes into $12$ — a question with no sensible answer.

The Limit Perspective
In calculus, we can ask: what happens to $\frac{1}{x}$ as $x \to 0$? From the right, it grows to $+\infty$. From the left, it falls to $-\infty$. Since there's no single value the limit approaches, we call $\frac{1}{0}$ undefined — not infinity, not zero.

Here's a classic example of what goes wrong when someone sneaks a division by zero into a proof:

Logical Fallacy — Fake Proof that $1 = 2$
proof.math — ⚠ CONTAINS ERROR
Step 1. Let $a = b$
Step 2. Then $a^2 = ab$
Step 3. Subtract $b^2$: $a^2 - b^2 = ab - b^2$
Step 4. Factor: $(a-b)(a+b) = b(a-b)$
Step 5. ✗ Cancel $(a - b)$: $a + b = b$
Step 6. Since $a = b$: $b + b = b$
∴ $2 = 1$     ❌ WRONG

# Error in Step 5: since a = b, we have (a - b) = 0.
# Dividing both sides by 0 is not allowed — it breaks math!

The hidden trap: since $a = b$, the factor $(a - b) = 0$. Cancelling it from both sides is equivalent to dividing by zero — which is why this "proof" produces nonsense.

Final Takeaway
From both an intuitive and formal standpoint, $\frac{a}{0}$ is undefined. Math's rule against it isn't arbitrary — it's what keeps our entire logical framework consistent. Violate it, and you can "prove" anything.
