Number Theory · Density of Primes

Why prime numbers become rarer as we count higher

An intuitive look at the Prime Number Theorem.

The Discovery

The Prime Number Theorem

If we let \(\pi(x)\) represent how many primes exist up to a number \(x\), the Prime Number Theorem tells us that this count follows a very specific trend as numbers get larger:

\[\pi(x)\approx\frac{x}{\log x}\]
The Intuition

Calculating the "Density"

To find the probability that a random number up to \(x\) is prime, we simply divide the number of primes by the total range of numbers:

\[\text{Density} = \frac{\pi(x)}{x} \approx \frac{1}{\log x}\]

Notice what happens as \(x\) grows toward infinity: the denominator (\(\log x\)) keeps getting bigger. This means the overall fraction gets closer and closer to zero.

\[\lim_{x\to\infty} (\text{Prime Density}) = 0\]
Up to 1 Million
\[\approx 1 \text{ in } 14 \text{ numbers}\]

Roughly 7.2% of numbers are prime.

Up to 1 Billion
\[\approx 1 \text{ in } 21 \text{ numbers}\]

The density drops to roughly 4.8%.
