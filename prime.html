Mathematics · The Density of Primes

Why prime numbers become rarer as they grow

A look at the mathematical rule governing the distribution of prime numbers.

The Core Rule

The Prime Number Theorem

To understand how primes are spread out, we use the function \(\pi(x)\), which simply counts how many primes exist up to a number \(x\). The Prime Number Theorem states that as \(x\) becomes very large:

\[\pi(x) \approx \frac{x}{\ln(x)}\]

This means the total count of primes is roughly the value of the number divided by its natural logarithm.

The Proof

Calculating the Density

The "density" of primes is the percentage of numbers that are prime within a certain range. We find this by dividing the count of primes \(\pi(x)\) by the total range of numbers \(x\):

\[\text{Density} = \frac{\pi(x)}{x} \approx \frac{1}{\ln(x)}\]

As the number \(x\) increases, its natural logarithm \(\ln(x)\) also increases. When you divide 1 by a number that keeps growing, the result gets smaller and smaller, eventually approaching zero:

\[\lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{1}{\ln(x)} = 0\]

This decreasing result proves that primes occupy a smaller and smaller fraction of the number line as we count higher.

At 1 Million (\(10^6\))

The density is about 1 in 13.8 numbers.

\[\approx 7.2\%\]
At 1 Billion (\(10^9\))

The density drops to 1 in 20.7 numbers.

\[\approx 4.8\%\]
